What happens when you atomise an 80-slide deck

3 September 2026 · 3 min read

Cognitive load theory says working memory is small. Breaking dense material into single-idea units respects that.

An 80-page lecture deck mixes definitions, worked examples, side notes and exam hints. For a student meeting the topic for the first time, that is a lot to hold at once.

Atomisation splits the material into concept units, each isolating one principle, linked to the ideas it depends on, and citing the slide, page or timestamp it came from. A student can master base cases before facing multi-step recursion problems.

Why the citations matter

Every unit points back to the original source. Students can check the lecturer's own wording, and faculty can see exactly how their material was interpreted.

Dual coding

Where a slide contains a diagram or formula, the unit keeps it alongside the text explanation, pairing verbal and visual representations rather than replacing one with the other.
